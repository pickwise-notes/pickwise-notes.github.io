How to choose a memory foam topper

Decide whether a memory foam topper suits your bed: how the foam behaves, heat, thickness, durability and alternatives.

What a memory foam topper can and can't do

A mattress topper is a thin layer that sits on top of a bed, generally around two to four inches thick. A memory foam topper is the version made from viscoelastic polyurethane, the material commonly called memory foam.[3][2]

People usually buy a topper for one of a few reasons: to soften a firm bed, to change how warm or cool the bed sleeps, to take wear off the more expensive base underneath, or to have something portable for a dorm or travel. Decide which of these is your real goal before comparing options, because each points toward different features.[2][3]

A topper works as an extra comfort layer, especially useful when the existing mattress is worn or uncomfortable. Because it is only a few inches thick, think of it as changing the surface feel rather than replacing a mattress.[3][2]

How the foam behaves

Memory foam is mostly polyurethane with added chemicals that raise its viscosity and density. Its cells are open, so air can move through the structure. Higher-density versions soften in response to body heat and mold to a warm body within a few minutes.[1]

The foam's behavior depends on temperature. If it gets too cold it stiffens, and if it gets too hot it acts more like ordinary foam and springs back quickly. A chilly bedroom may therefore make a topper feel firmer at first than it will once your body has warmed it.[1]

The slow, gradual recovery comes partly from air needing time to flow back into the pores, and smaller pores slow that airflow further. Newer foams may recover their shape faster. Feel can therefore vary quite a bit between one memory foam topper and another, even when the material sounds the same.[1]

Sleeping warm: what the cooling features are meant to do

Trapped heat can be a drawback of memory foam in mattresses and pillows. Later generations of the material used a more open cell structure to improve breathability.[1]

Gel-infused foam, which fuses gel particles into the foam, was developed to reduce trapped body heat. A later variant used capsules of phase-change material, which absorb heat by changing from solid to liquid. Some makers also use moisture-wicking fabrics or phase-change materials in the covers.[1]

These designs are intended to reduce heat, but there are no figures here showing how much cooler any version actually sleeps. Treat cooling claims as aims rather than guarantees. If you run hot, look for specific details about the cell structure, the cover fabric and the airflow rather than a general promise.[1][3]

A topper can make a bed either warmer or cooler depending on its design. Airflow and heat-conductive materials push it one way, while dense, close-contouring foam may push it the other. Your own tendency to sleep hot or cold should drive this choice.[3][1]

Thickness, size and fit

Toppers usually fall in a fairly narrow thickness range. Thinner and lower-density ones are lighter and easier to carry, which is why they suit dorms and travel. A thicker topper changes the feel of the bed more, but it is also bulkier to move and store.[3][2]

Toppers are usually held in place with straps or elasticated corners. Mattress sizes follow standards that vary by market, and names such as Queen, Full or Single stand for specific dimensions. Measure your bed and confirm that the size label matches before you order, and check that the fastening method works with your bedding.[2]

Most toppers are sold for use on top of a box spring or similar base. One stated purpose is to extend the life of that more expensive base, so think about what is underneath yours.[2][3]

Comfort versus durability

There is a trade-off between comfort and durability in memory foam. Some foams have a more rigid cell structure, which spreads weight less evenly but recovers its shape better and tends to last longer. A denser cell structure also resists water vapor, which can help the foam wear better and keep its appearance.[1]

That means the softest, most contouring foam is not automatically the one that will hold up longest. If you want a topper to last, look for information on density and construction, not only on how plush it is meant to feel.[1]

Memory foam toppers are often marketed as improving support and spinal alignment. These claims are touted rather than proven, so weigh them with care. Memory foam is also regularly used in medical settings to relieve pressure, which fits the foam's pressure-relieving character.[3][1]

Other topper types worth comparing

Memory foam is only one option. Toppers are also made from wool, cotton, feathers and latex. Pillow-top styles use fibers such as cotton, down or microfiber, usually divided into pockets so the filling stays put, and they tend to be about four inches thick or more.[3]

Latex comes from rubber tree sap and feels springy and responsive. There are two main manufacturing methods. One produces a heavier foam that is mostly used for support layers. The other, made by flash freezing, is lighter and fluffier, lacks support, and is used mainly as a comfort layer. If you dislike the slow sink of memory foam, latex is a different kind of feel.[3]

Protection, cleaning and allergies

A topper is not the same as a mattress protector. A protector is a thin, generally unpadded layer meant to shield the mattress, while a topper is meant to change comfort. Some toppers can be machine washed, but covers and fillings differ, so check the care instructions.[3][4]

Perspiration passes easily through an ordinary sheet and stains the mattress surface. Most mattress warranties are voided by a stain, so a separate protector is worth considering. Read your own warranty terms to see what applies to you.[4]

For people with dust mite allergies, a fully zippered encasement is the recommended form of protection. A 2002 Norwegian study found that foam mattresses were four to eight times more likely than spring mattresses to host dust mite feces. That finding concerns mattresses, not toppers, but allergy-prone sleepers may still want to bear it in mind.[4]

Plastic waterproof pads can be hot to sleep on and can alter the feel of a bed. A breathable laminate that is not stretched tight is described as a gentler alternative.[4]

Quick checklist

  • Write down your main goal first: softer feel, temperature change, protecting the base, or portability.[2][3]
  • Measure your bed and confirm that the size label and fastening method fit it.[2]
  • Choose a thickness that fits how much change you want and whether you need to carry it.[3]
  • Look for density and construction details, remembering that the softest foam is not always the most durable.[1]
  • If you sleep hot, look for specifics on cell structure, cover fabric and airflow, and treat cooling claims as unproven.[1][3]
  • Check the care instructions to see whether the cover or topper can be machine washed.[3]
  • Read your mattress warranty and consider a separate protector, or a zippered encasement if you have allergies.[4]

Questions

Will a memory foam topper fix a worn-out mattress?

It can add a comfort layer to a worn or uncomfortable bed, but it is only a few inches thick. It mainly changes the surface feel, so don't expect it to replace a mattress.[3][2]

Do memory foam toppers sleep hot?

They can. Heat retention is a known drawback of the material. Gel infusions, open-cell structures and special covers are meant to reduce it, but there are no figures on how well they work, so look for specific details.[1]

Is a topper the same as a mattress protector?

No. A protector is a thin layer meant to shield the mattress, and it may also guard against allergens. A topper is meant to change comfort. Some products blur the terms, so read the product description.[3][4]

What are the alternatives to memory foam?

Common options include pillow-top styles filled with fibers such as cotton, down or microfiber, as well as wool and latex. Latex feels springier and more responsive than memory foam.[3]
